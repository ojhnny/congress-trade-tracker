STOCK Act filing audit

Members must disclose a trade within 45 days of making it, or within 30 days of learning about it if a spouse or adviser made it. Filings more than 30 days past the deadline carry a $200 late fee.

    How this works

    Every number on this site comes from public disclosures. Read the findings as patterns. A trade in an industry a member oversees is not evidence that the member misused information.

    Sources

    • House Clerk financial disclosuresPeriodic transaction reports, some filed as scanned PDFs
    • Senate electronic financial disclosuresPeriodic transaction reports
    • congress-legislatorsMembers and committee assignments, with dates
    • SEC EDGARTickers, company names and SIC industry codes
    • Daily pricesClosing prices for each stock and for SPY, the benchmark

    Amounts are ranges

    Members report each trade as one of ten value bands, not an exact amount. The site keeps the band and draws it as a ten-step scale, so you always see what was actually disclosed.

    Totals are shown as the sum of every band's floor to the sum of every band's ceiling. A midpoint is used only to sort members, never as a reported figure.

    What counts as overlap

    Each stock is assigned an industry from its SEC SIC code. A hand-built table maps each industry to the House and Senate committees with jurisdiction over it. A trade is an overlap when the member sat on one of those committees on the trade date.

    The expected rate answers the question "how often would this happen by chance?". For every trade, it takes the share of all congressional trading that falls in the member's overseen industries, then averages across trades.

    Returns

    Returns are measured from the trade date close to 30 and 90 days later, minus SPY over the same window. Sales are measured the same way, so a positive number after a sale means the stock kept rising.
